What happens when the government mandates electric vans, but the market cannot afford to buy them?

📝 Articles 24 Nov 2026

Britain's van manufacturers are facing an increasingly difficult challenge. Under the Government's Zero Emission Vehicle (ZEV) mandate, manufacturers face a headline target equivalent to 24% of new van sales being zero-emission in 2026, rising to 34% in 2027 and 70% by 2030.

In 2025, battery-electric vans accounted for just 8.7% of new registrations for vehicles up to 3.5 tonnes, falling considerably short of the 16% mandate target. The situation has improved during 2026, but progress remains slow.

By October, electric vans up to 3.5 tonnes accounted for just 9.7% of registrations year-to-date, against a target of 24%. Even including heavier electric commercial vehicles, the overall LCV figure reached only 11%, with vans up to 3.5 tonnes remaining below 10%.

The problem isn't simply that businesses are reluctant to go electric. Purchase prices remain high, charging infrastructure is poorly suited to working vans, and depreciation can make leasing costs difficult to justify. Manufacturers are being asked to deliver a transition that their customers cannot always afford or accommodate.

So, with 2030 approaching, is Britain heading towards an electric van revolution, or a commercial vehicle crisis?

For tradespeople, rural contractors, and regional logistics operators, the arithmetic still fails to stack up:

Upfront Capital Cost: Even with the Plug-in Van Grant (£2,500 for small vans; £5,000 for large vans), electric equivalents can carry a £10,000–£15,000 price premium over diesel counterparts.

Operational Friction: Cold-weather range degradation, heavy payloads, and a public charging network designed for passenger cars rather than long-wheelbase vans make single-shift regional work difficult without depot infrastructure.

Residual Uncertainty: Weak used demand for first-generation electric vans has left leasing companies pricing steep depreciation into monthly rentals.


Despite the widening gap between electric van registrations and the mandated targets, manufacturers have not simply been left to pay penalties on every missing electric vehicle.

The ZEV mandate contains a series of compliance mechanisms that allow manufacturers to trade allowances, carry credits forward from previous years, borrow against future allocations and, in certain circumstances, transfer credits between the car and van schemes.

These provisions have helped soften the immediate financial impact of the transition, although they do not resolve the underlying problem of weak electric van demand.

Under the Vehicle Emissions Trading Schemes (VETS) rules, brands have stayed compliant through four principal compliance mechanisms:

Banking Previous Allowances: Manufacturers that outperform their ZEV targets can carry surplus allowances forward, providing a reserve that can be used to help meet future compliance obligations.

Borrowing Against Future Years: Manufacturers can borrow allowances from future compliance periods, subject to regulatory limits. These allowances must be repaid by 2030, with interest applied to the borrowed balance.

Trading and Cross-Scheme Transfers: Manufacturers can acquire allowances from other participants or, where the regulations permit, trade allowances between the car and van schemes. One car-scheme allowance can be exchanged for 0.4 van-scheme credits, providing an additional route for manufacturers with surplus car allowances to meet their van obligations.

CO₂ Allowance Conversions: Manufacturers can also use qualifying surplus allowances from their vehicle emissions trading schemes to offset ZEV mandate shortfalls. These conversions are subject to annual limits and fixed exchange rates, providing another route towards compliance without requiring every missing electric vehicle to be replaced by a direct purchase of credits.


The wider danger for British businesses is not merely higher van prices; it is structural supply withdrawal.

Right-hand-drive commercial vehicles already represent a relatively small, bespoke engineering run for European manufacturers. If Westminster insists on enforcing unachievable quotas backed by £15,000 fines, global OEMs will simply cap UK diesel imports rather than subsidise loss-making sales—leaving British fleets facing acute shortages and inflated used values.

Nowhere is this tension sharper than at Ellesmere Port. Following the closure of Vauxhall's Luton manufacturing facility in 2025, the Cheshire plant became Stellantis's sole remaining UK vehicle assembly factory. It also occupies a particularly important position within the group's electric vehicle strategy, having been established as Stellantis's first dedicated battery-electric vehicle manufacturing facility.

The site already produces compact electric vans for Vauxhall, Peugeot, Citroën and Fiat Professional, while Stellantis is investing in production of larger battery-electric commercial vehicles. The longer-term plan is to establish Ellesmere Port as an all-electric UK light commercial vehicle manufacturing hub.

Its future therefore has significance beyond a single factory: it represents a substantial part of Britain's remaining commercial vehicle manufacturing capacity.


The Government's review of the ZEV mandate has now reached a critical stage. A joint consultation involving the UK Government and devolved administrations closed on 23 October 2026, inviting views on how the regulations should operate in the years ahead. The Government has said it intends to publish its response by the end of 2026, with any changes potentially taking effect from the 2027 compliance year.

For manufacturers and fleet operators, the outcome could prove significant. The 2027 van target is scheduled to rise to 34%, yet electric van registrations remain substantially below that level. Whether ministers retain the existing trajectory, introduce additional flexibility or revise elements of the scheme remains to be seen.

Britain's electric van transition is not necessarily doomed. Battery technology is improving, charging infrastructure is expanding and manufacturers are introducing increasingly capable electric commercial vehicles. But the timetable must reflect the realities of the businesses expected to buy them.

The challenge facing ministers is simple: electric van adoption is rising, but the mandate is increasing much faster. Unless costs fall and infrastructure improves significantly, the gap highlighted above may continue widening even as more businesses switch to electric vehicles.

Britain's electric van transition is no longer a question of technology. It is increasingly a question of timing.
