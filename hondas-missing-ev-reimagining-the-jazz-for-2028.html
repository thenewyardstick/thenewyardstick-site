Honda's Missing EV? Reimagining the Jazz for 2028

✎ Design Studio 7 Nov 2026

Honda's next major electric arrival is the ALPHA, a compact SUV first previewed at the 2025 Japan Mobility Show. Built in India for global markets, its production version is scheduled to make its debut on 2 December 2026, with sales planned for 2027, initially focusing on India and Japan.

Official specifications remain under wraps, although a length of approximately 4.3–4.4 metres, front-wheel drive and battery options potentially ranging from 65–75kWh are anticipated.

For the Jazz, the ALPHA presents an intriguing possibility. If its electric architecture proves flexible enough, could Honda develop a shorter derivative for its established European supermini? Reducing the overall length to around 4.1 metres and introducing smaller 44–55kWh batteries could create an appealing alternative to the Renault 4.

With this in mind, TNY Design Studio has imagined how a fifth-generation Honda Jazz EV might look in 2028, combining familiar practicality with styling inspired by Honda's latest electric models.


Our imagined Jazz EV deliberately avoids the increasingly fashionable crossover look. At approximately 4.1 metres long, it retains the Jazz's upright silhouette, generous glass area and short bonnet, making the most of its compact footprint. A simple, modern front end introduces a little of Honda's Japanese kei-car character, without turning the Jazz into a miniature SUV.

That emphasis on practicality has always been part of the Jazz's appeal, and electrification needn't change it. We also see little reason to abandon the existing Jazz e:HEV immediately. An electric model could initially sit alongside today's efficient hybrid, giving customers two compact, genuinely practical Hondas to suit different lifestyles.

The challenge would be preserving the flexible seating, easy access and clever interior packaging that have made the Jazz such an enduring favourite. With so many small EVs chasing style, there's certainly room for one that puts space first.


Honda's new Super-N demonstrates just how much practicality can be squeezed into a tiny electric car. Measuring only 3.6 metres long, the Japanese kei-inspired hatchback accommodates four adults, features Honda's ingenious Magic Seats and offers remarkable versatility for its size.

Starting at £18,995, it isn't outrageously expensive either, particularly considering its generous equipment and distinctive character. Nevertheless, its 29.6kWh battery provides just 128 miles of WLTP combined range. That's perfectly reasonable for urban commuting or use as a second car, but considerably limits its appeal to drivers needing one vehicle for everything.

Competition is increasingly difficult to ignore, too. The Leapmotor T03 offers 165 miles of range for just £12,995. Meanwhile, larger electric alternatives offer considerably more space and range for around the same money.

This is precisely where an electric Jazz could make sense, combining Honda's clever approach to interior packaging with the space, battery capacity and range needed for everyday family motoring.


Honda's recent electric ventures haven't quite managed to replicate the success of its established hybrid models. The charming Honda e certainly looked the part, but its premium pricing and modest 137-mile WLTP range limited its appeal. The subsequent e:Ny1 SUV offered greater practicality, but with a launch price of £44,995 and a 256-mile range, it struggled to stand out against competitive European alternatives.

Honda closed UK orders for the e:Ny1 earlier in 2026, leaving the new Super-N as its only currently available electric passenger car in Britain.

That creates an increasingly difficult situation. The UK's zero-emission vehicle target rises from 38% in 2027 to 52% in 2028, before climbing to 66% in 2029. Meeting the 2028 target entirely through vehicle sales would require Honda to register more EVs than petrol and hybrid cars combined.

Manufacturers can purchase credits and use other regulatory flexibilities, but Honda faces an important decision: continue relying on such mechanisms or develop a broader electric range capable of attracting British and European buyers.

An electric Jazz, potentially joined by the ALPHA, could provide part of that answer.


The Civic has long been regarded as one of Honda's defining models, but British sales figures tell a surprisingly different story.

Since the Jazz arrived in 2002, approximately 460,000 examples have been registered in the UK through to the end of 2025. Over exactly the same period, the Civic managed around 483,000, just 5% more. In fact, the Jazz has outsold its more celebrated stablemate every year since 2009.

Across four generations, the Jazz has quietly become one of Honda's most important European nameplates, winning buyers through practicality, reliability and an ability to feel much bigger inside than its dimensions suggest. The Civic might attract more headlines, particularly in performance form, but the Jazz has played an equally important role in building Honda's loyal customer base.

Its future therefore deserves serious consideration, and an electric version could help ensure this remarkably successful little car remains relevant for another generation.
