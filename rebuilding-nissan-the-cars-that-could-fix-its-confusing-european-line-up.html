Rebuilding Nissan: The Cars That Could Fix Its Confusing European Line-Up

📝 Articles 30 Aug 2026

Around 20 years ago, Nissan changed the shape of the mainstream car market. The Qashqai replaced the dependable but forgettable Almera and helped turn the crossover into a family-car staple. The Juke followed, proving equally disruptive, while in 2010 the Leaf brought electric motoring to the masses.

All three were game-changing cars - and each proved more successful than Nissan initially anticipated. Today, however, the headlines are very different: financial pressure, shrinking model ranges and, most recently, reports of plans for an electric Qashqai being dropped. So, what could turn things around?

At the bottom of Nissan’s new range, the Micra EV is refreshingly easy to understand. Renault has provided strong foundations, but while the closely related Renault 5 majors on retro desirability, the Micra has developed its own USP: value. With the Renault’s £21,495 post-grant price proving difficult to discount, brand-new Micras can already be found for under £19,000, making this a compelling entry point to new-generation EV technology.

It also introduces an oddity in Nissan’s hierarchy. The Micra offers 1,106 litres of luggage capacity with its seats folded, versus 1,052 litres in the considerably larger Leaf. The Leaf remains stylish, exceptionally efficient and technically impressive, but its slippery shape comes at a price. Nissan’s smallest EV unexpectedly highlights why clearer roles further up the range are needed. Photo credit: Nissan

The Micra isn’t the only model raising questions about the Leaf’s place in Nissan’s hierarchy. The forthcoming Juke EV will sit below it on price, wrapping electric technology in a more upright small-SUV body. Nissan hasn’t released final luggage figures, so it’s too early to declare the Juke more practical, but the signs point that way. Today's Juke offers substantially more maximum luggage capacity than the new Leaf, and Nissan says its electric successor will be roomier still.

Alongside the cheaper Micra, it means Nissan could soon have two EVs beneath the £28,849 Leaf offering more conventional shapes and strong everyday usability. That doesn’t make the Leaf a poor proposition. Its USP is increasingly clear: exceptional efficiency, long range and sleek styling. But those qualities come at a price - financially and in outright versatility - making a clearly defined hierarchy increasingly important. Photo credit: Nissan


The Ariya remains an appealing, comfortable and distinctive EV, but as Nissan’s electric flagship it simply doesn’t create enough distance from the cars below it. At 4,595mm long, with a 466-litre boot and 1,350 litres with the seats folded, its practicality is relatively modest. Comparable electric SUVs demonstrate what is possible: the 4,650mm Vauxhall Grandland Electric offers 550 litres, expanding to 1,645 litres, while the MG S6 EV provides a huge 674 litres and 1,910 litres respectively.

None of this makes the Ariya a bad car; it simply feels undersized for the role it has inherited. Our second-generation Ariya moves decisively upwards, creating the space and separation a true Nissan flagship deserves. Image credit: TheNewYardstick Design Studio

Nissan’s current financial pressures mean simply adding another expensive halo EV above the Ariya is unlikely to be the answer. The cancelled electric Qashqai might once have provided the practical family EV to complement the sleeker Leaf, but that now leaves Ariya with a much bigger role to fill. Our solution is to move it upwards. Today’s Ariya is actually smaller than the X-Trail, despite serving as one of Nissan’s most aspirational models in Europe.

The next generation should offer substantially more space, while keeping pricing competitive enough not to abandon existing Ariya buyers. Nissan has already demonstrated the importance of value, cutting Ariya prices substantially in 2025. Our larger Ariya Mk2 builds on that repositioning: more flagship, rather than simply more expensive. Image credit: TheNewYardstick Design Studio


Nissan’s current crossover hierarchy is easy to understand: Juke, Qashqai and X-Trail provide three distinct steps in size and price. But the Juke is now approaching the end of a long life. It remains one of Nissan’s most distinctive designs, yet today’s limited powertrain choice lacks some of the character of earlier generations, when buyers could choose petrol, diesel and even 200hp Nismo derivatives.

With the next-generation Juke moving to electric power, Nissan has an opportunity to rethink what eventually replaces today’s hybrid model. Our solution is Note X: roughly Juke-sized, but more practical, powered by e-POWER technology and priced deliberately below Qashqai. It would preserve an affordable hybrid entry point while allowing the new Juke EV to develop a much clearer identity of its own. Photo credit: Nissan

Our Note X captures some of the thinking behind the original Qashqai and Note: practical, efficient and well-priced family cars that made clever use of relatively compact dimensions. At around Juke size, Note X would prioritise space and usability over style-led packaging, providing an affordable hybrid step beneath Qashqai. Nissan already has suitable technology, with its hybrids producing from around 140hp and Qashqai e-POWER reaching 190hp - comfortably beyond today’s similarly sized Juke.

It wouldn’t need to be revolutionary. Affordable, economical and genuinely practical, Note X could simply give Nissan another easy-to-understand family car, while allowing the new Juke to begin its next chapter as an EV.

Nissan already has plenty of good cars. What its European range increasingly lacks is a completely obvious hierarchy. Our solution adds little complexity: Note X provides the practical hybrid entry point, while a larger Ariya becomes the unmistakable flagship. Between them, Nissan’s existing models are free to play to their individual strengths.

Twenty years after Qashqai changed Nissan’s fortunes, perhaps the answer isn’t another revolution - just making every model’s purpose crystal clear. Image credit: TheNewYardstick Design Studio
