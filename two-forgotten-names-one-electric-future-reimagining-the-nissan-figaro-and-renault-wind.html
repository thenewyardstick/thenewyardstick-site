Two Forgotten Names, One Electric Future – Reimagining the Nissan Figaro and Renault Wind

✎ Design Studio 27 Oct 2026

The new Renault Twingo E-Tech electric represents an important step towards making small electric cars genuinely affordable.

Measuring just 3.79 metres long, it combines a 27.5kWh LFP battery with an 82hp electric motor, delivering up to 163 miles of WLTP range. Its five-door body, sliding rear seats and surprisingly versatile interior demonstrate just how much practicality can be squeezed into a genuinely compact car.

Yet the Twingo's greatest potential may extend beyond Renault's own showrooms. Its adapted AmpR Small architecture provides a modern electric foundation that could be shared across several models, reducing development costs while allowing manufacturers to create vehicles with very different identities.


The original Renault Wind was a rare attempt to create a small roadster using everyday supermini engineering. Our imagined electric successor takes a similar approach, using the new Twingo as its starting point but giving the result a very different personality.

The two-seat cabin, short rear deck and pronounced rear haunches create a compact coupé shape, while the short sloping roof provides a visual link to the earlier car. At the front, rounded LED headlights and a slim grille bring the Wind into Renault's current design language, complemented by clean bodywork and distinctive electric alloy wheels.

We wouldn't expect a car like this to chase enormous performance figures. A modest increase over the standard Twingo's output, alongside carefully tuned suspension, could be enough to make it genuinely entertaining while keeping weight and costs under control.


A revived Wind should embrace colour. The Twingo has always worn its playful character openly, and a compact two-seat EV would look rather lost in a range limited to black, white and grey. Our four-car study shows how much the design changes with little more than paint.

For something designed to be fun rather than essential family transport, that matters. An electric Wind should be small enough to be sensible, but playful enough to make its owner smile.


Launched in 2010, the Renault Wind squeezed the idea of an open-top sports car into a body just 3.83 metres long. Sharing its foundations with the contemporary Twingo, it offered a 100hp 1.2-litre turbocharged petrol engine or a livelier 133hp 1.6-litre, both paired with manual gearboxes.

Its cleverest feature was a single-piece metal roof that rotated backwards in just 12 seconds, leaving the 270-litre boot space unaffected. British prices started at approximately £15,500, making it a relatively accessible little roadster.

The later Gordini edition added further visual character, but the Wind never achieved widespread popularity. Nevertheless, it brought something refreshingly different to the small-car market.


There is, of course, a commercial question: would enough people buy a two-seat electric Wind to justify bringing it back? The original demonstrated how limited that audience can be, and developing an entirely bespoke successor would be difficult to justify.

Sharing the Twingo's battery, motor and electronic systems could help change that equation, particularly if Renault resisted the temptation to make the car unnecessarily complicated.

An electric Wind wouldn't need to become the brand's next bestseller. It could offer something increasingly uncommon: an attainable little car bought for the pleasure of owning and driving it, rather than maximum practicality.

And Renault isn't alone in having a distinctive small car whose story might not be finished.


Nissan's new Pixo demonstrates how its long-standing partnership with Renault continues to deliver new models, with their collaboration now extending into Europe's smallest electric cars.

Revealed in September 2026, the compact EV shares much of its engineering with the new Twingo, including a 27.5kWh LFP battery and 82hp electric motor, offering up to 161 miles of WLTP range. Set to be produced by Renault in Slovenia, it gives Nissan an affordable entry point beneath the larger Micra EV.

Interestingly, TNY Design Studio published our own Nissan Pixo EV rendering on 13 September, ten days before Nissan revealed the production car's design.

Our exploration of this emerging small-EV family extends considerably further. Back in August, we imagined three possible directions for the next Dacia Spring, before Dacia officially unveiled its second-generation model in September.

We've also explored an affordable Alpine A190 electric hot hatch, and even a modern Mitsubishi i for Europe.

With the Pixo and Spring now revealed, these independent Design Studio explorations demonstrate just how much variety shared electric engineering could potentially support. And with Nissan bringing the Pixo name back, perhaps another distinctive little car from its past deserves consideration.


Where our Renault Wind EV takes a sportier approach, the Nissan Figaro embraces nostalgia. TNY Design Studio imagines the two cars as close mechanical relatives, sharing the Twingo's electric motor, battery and as much underlying engineering as practical. Producing them alongside one another could help make two distinctive, relatively low-volume models more financially viable.

Visually, however, the Figaro would be unmistakably different. Our rendering retains the original's charming proportions, rounded headlights and delicate chrome detailing, complemented by a contrasting cream metal roof. Pastel green bodywork, small amber indicators and cream-coloured wheels with polished hubcaps complete the retro appearance.

We've deliberately avoided oversized wheels, aggressive bumpers or unnecessary styling flourishes. The Figaro's appeal was always its individuality, and an electric successor should preserve that character.

Where the Pixo provides Nissan with practical, affordable electric transport, a revived Figaro could offer something rather more emotional: a small car people buy simply because they love it.


First shown at the 1989 Tokyo Motor Show, the Nissan Figaro arrived in 1991 as one of Nissan's imaginative Pike Factory creations. Beneath its 1950s-inspired bodywork were first-generation Micra mechanicals, including a turbocharged 987cc petrol engine producing 76PS, paired with a three-speed automatic gearbox.

Measuring just 3.74 metres long, the Figaro combined a folding fabric roof with cream leather upholstery, chrome detailing and four pastel colours inspired by the seasons. Production was limited to approximately 20,000 examples, with demand so strong that prospective buyers were selected by lottery.

Although originally sold exclusively in Japan, many subsequently found their way to Britain, where the Figaro developed an enthusiastic following.

More than three decades later, its enduring appeal demonstrates something rather important: a small car doesn't need impressive performance figures to become genuinely desirable.


The Renault Wind and Nissan Figaro might seem unlikely candidates for an electric revival, but both demonstrate how much character can be created from relatively ordinary small-car engineering.

Neither would be an obvious volume seller. A two-seat electric coupé and a retro-inspired Nissan would appeal to buyers looking for something beyond everyday practicality, and their commercial success would depend heavily on keeping development and production costs under control.

That is where the new Twingo's foundations become particularly interesting. Sharing batteries, motors and other components across several models could allow manufacturers to experiment with distinctive designs without developing entirely new cars from scratch.

Our imagined Wind and Figaro take two very different approaches to that opportunity: one playful and sporting, the other charming and nostalgic. Neither has been announced for production, but perhaps the next generation of affordable electric cars could offer more than sensible five-door hatchbacks.

After all, there should still be room for cars that make people smile.
