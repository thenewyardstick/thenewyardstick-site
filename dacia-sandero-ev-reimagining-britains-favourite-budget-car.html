Dacia Sandero EV – Reimagining Britain's Favourite Budget Car

✎ Design Studio 22 Nov 2026

When Dacia arrived in Britain, it was the Sandero that led the charge, offering motorists something increasingly difficult to find: straightforward, attainable new-car ownership without unnecessary complications.

More than a decade later, the Romanian brand has grown into a six-model line-up, stretching from the compact Spring and Sandero to the more adventurous Sandero Stepway, Duster, Jogger and flagship Bigster, with the forthcoming Striker set to broaden its appeal further.

Dacia has built a loyal following by concentrating on everyday usability, value and honest motoring, rather than chasing premium aspirations. Now, as the industry moves towards electrification, we imagine how the Sandero could evolve into an accessible electric supermini.

Our vision retains the familiar no-nonsense character, but introduces a sharper, more contemporary design that could help bring affordable electric motoring to an even wider audience.


When the second-generation Sandero arrived in Britain in 2013, its headline price was just £5,995 in basic specification. That was a remarkable proposition, but the economics of building small, affordable cars have become considerably more challenging.

Inflation is only part of the story: increasingly demanding safety requirements, emissions legislation and the technology expected by modern buyers have all added costs, with smaller cars having far less room to absorb them. Today's Sandero starts at £14,765, while Dacia's Spring electric car undercuts it at £11,990.

The Spring offers another advantage for Renault Group, too. Its zero-emission registrations contribute towards manufacturer ZEV targets, with compliance mechanisms allowing credits and overperformance to be shared or traded.

In an increasingly regulated market, a small, relatively simple electric car could prove valuable both commercially and strategically.


There may be another opportunity for Dacia in Renault Group's developing electric-car architecture. The proposed European M1E category for smaller, value-led electric vehicles could eventually provide additional regulatory incentives, although its final rules and eligibility requirements remain important.

Dacia has already shown interest in the idea through its Hipster concept. For the Sandero, however, the bigger opportunity could be adapting existing Renault Group technology without inheriting all the expense of a more sophisticated electric model.

A smaller 27.5kWh LFP battery, as used in the Twingo, could create a genuinely city-focused entry-level version. The 36.5kWh LFP pack introduced in the entry-level Renault 5 in some European markets offers a more substantial alternative.

Alternatively, the reported 44kWh battery could provide considerably more versatility. With careful specification and cost control, Dacia could offer several levels of electric Sandero without losing sight of its central promise: affordable motoring.

If Dacia could deliver an electric Sandero from around £15,000-£18,000 while preserving genuine family-car space, it would occupy a largely uncontested space in the market.


We have also imagined how Dacia could recreate the spirit of the original Sandero Access for the electric age. Rather than filling the cabin with increasingly expensive screens and technology, our entry-level proposal takes a simpler approach.

Basic steel wheels, unpainted plastic mirror caps, conventional physical controls and a smartphone-based infotainment system could remove considerable hardware and cost. Owners would use their own devices for navigation, music and connectivity, rather than paying for a large touchscreen and associated electronics.

The Twingo's 80hp electric motor would also be more than adequate for a lightweight, city-focused Sandero, with a smaller battery helping keep the purchase price under control.

The objective would be to create a genuinely affordable alternative to the increasingly fashionable, technology-heavy Renault 5, bringing the original Sandero's accessible approach to a new generation of buyers.


Dacia has demonstrated that it can challenge larger manufacturers at the upper end of the affordable market, with well-considered models such as the Bigster and forthcoming Striker expanding its appeal without abandoning its value-led philosophy.

But perhaps its greatest opportunity lies at the opposite end of the scale. The Spring has opened the door to electric motoring at a lower price, although its compact dimensions inevitably limit its appeal for some buyers.

The Sandero could occupy that crucial middle ground, offering the space, practicality and everyday usability that made the original such a success, while bringing electric power within reach of a much wider audience.

It was the Sandero that helped establish Dacia's reputation in Britain. As Renault Group continues expanding its electric ambitions, perhaps it is also the Sandero that can help secure the brand's next chapter.
