Reinventing the Lancia Delta: From Everyday Elegance to HF Performance

✎ Design Studio 31 Oct 2026

For our vision of a fourth-generation Lancia Delta, The New Yardstick Design Studio has taken inspiration from the latest Ypsilon, developing its distinctive styling into a larger, more substantial C-segment family hatchback.

Measuring approximately 4.4 metres in length, our interpretation retains Lancia's modern lighting signature, contrasting black roof and sculpted bodywork, while introducing a longer body and a stronger, more mature profile. The distinctive three-spoke alloy wheels draw inspiration from Lancia's striking Pu+Ra HPE concept of 2023, providing a further connection to the brand's evolving design language and rich heritage.

The original Delta arrived in 1979, followed by a second generation in 1993 and a considerably more luxurious third generation in 2008. Each brought something different to the nameplate, from understated Italian practicality to distinctive styling and premium aspirations.

Our proposed fourth generation aims to bring those qualities together, creating a modern, characterful alternative to today's conventional family hatchbacks.


The Ypsilon was the natural starting point for Lancia's revival. Its heritage stretches back to the Y10 of 1985, beginning a successful line of stylish Italian small cars that attracted more than three million buyers. For years, the previous Ypsilon kept Lancia alive almost single-handedly in Italy, making its replacement the logical first step towards rebuilding the brand.

The latest generation introduces modern styling, technology and petrol, hybrid and electric powertrains. Italian prices start at €22,700 for the petrol, €25,200 for the hybrid and €35,400 for the electric version. 

Next comes the Gamma, unveiled in 2026. This 4.67-metre fastback crossover takes Lancia into a larger, more premium segment, offering a 560-litre boot and hybrid or electric powertrains. Italian prices start at €39,900 for the hybrid and €45,900 for the electric version, rising to €59,900 for the flagship 375bhp Gamma HF Integrale.

For a revived Delta, the obvious comparisons are the Peugeot 308 and DS N°4. Both occupy the 4.4-metre family hatchback class, where our imagined Delta could combine everyday practicality with distinctive Italian styling and a more premium character.

From the rear, our interpretation of the fourth-generation Delta introduces a little more individuality. The circular rear lights draw inspiration from Lancia's Ypsilon, while a broad black panel across the tailgate gives the car a distinctly modern appearance.

The longer roofline and relatively upright tailgate reinforce its role as a practical family hatchback. At approximately 4.4 metres long, it would offer considerably more space than the Ypsilon without stepping into the larger Gamma's territory.

The result is deliberately understated: a distinctive Italian alternative to familiar European family cars, designed to make the Delta name relevant to a new generation.


Of course, bringing back the Delta name raises another question: could Lancia resist creating a new HF?

Our interpretation takes the regular Delta and gives it a more purposeful appearance, with wider wheel arches, a deeper front bumper and subtle performance-inspired detailing. The result is sporty without losing the elegance expected of a modern Lancia.

We would put our money on an entirely electric Delta HF, with two obvious possibilities already available within Lancia's expanding range. The first would follow the Ypsilon HF, using a 280bhp front-wheel-drive powertrain. The more ambitious alternative would borrow the Gamma HF Integrale's 375bhp all-wheel-drive arrangement.

The former could deliver an accessible performance hatchback, while the latter would create a genuine flagship. Neither has been confirmed for the Delta, but both provide an intriguing foundation for its potential return.


Few performance hatchbacks carry the reputation of the original Delta HF Integrale. Developed from the first-generation Delta, introduced in 1979, its four-wheel-drive rally derivatives became motorsport legends. Between 1987 and 1992, Lancia secured six consecutive World Rally Championship manufacturers' titles, establishing a record that remains central to the brand's identity.

Yet Lancia's relationship with Britain took a very different direction. The marque officially withdrew from the UK market in 1994, although two familiar names briefly returned in disguise. From 2011, British buyers could purchase the Lancia Delta and Ypsilon wearing Chrysler badges instead.

That unusual arrangement was short-lived. The Delta disappeared from the range, and Chrysler announced its withdrawal from Britain in 2015.

More than three decades after Lancia's original departure, Britain still lacks an official Lancia showroom. But for anyone interested in owning the latest Ypsilon, there might be another way.

Britain might not have a Lancia dealership, but that doesn't mean the new Ypsilon is out of reach. We found an October 2026 petrol Ypsilon in Italy, with just five kilometres recorded, advertised for €16,900. The hybrid and electric examples we found were €1,000 and €3,000 more expensive respectively.

Manufactured in Zaragoza, Spain, the Ypsilon should qualify for tariff-free entry into Britain with the appropriate proof of European origin. If the dealer removes Italian VAT for export, the petrol model could cost approximately £14,000, including UK import VAT and basic registration fees, but excluding transport.

Our research hasn't uncovered a confirmed British road-registered example of this generation, suggesting an import would potentially be the first.

Although left-hand drive, servicing should be relatively straightforward thanks to shared Stellantis mechanicals with the Vauxhall Corsa and Peugeot 208, although specialist parts and warranty support deserve consideration. For roughly Corsa money, it's a fascinating opportunity to own something genuinely different.

While the Ypsilon offers an intriguing way for British enthusiasts to experience Lancia's revival today, the company's return to rallying provides another tantalising glimpse of what its future could hold — particularly for a reborn Delta HF.

That possibility inspired our own Delta HF Rally2 interpretation. We've taken the proportions of our proposed 4.4-metre family hatchback and introduced wider bodywork, competition wheels, enlarged air intakes and a prominent rear wing, creating something that feels unmistakably connected to Lancia's rallying heritage.

The design remains entirely speculative, with no Delta competition programme announced. However, Lancia's motorsport ambitions are no longer confined to heritage celebrations and concept sketches.

The new Ypsilon has already established a genuine rallying programme, with several competition versions catering for everything from aspiring drivers to professional teams competing internationally. It's an encouraging development for a manufacturer whose greatest achievements were forged on rally stages.


Lancia's rallying revival begins with the Ypsilon HF Racing, an entry-level Rally6 car powered by a 145bhp 1.2-litre turbocharged petrol engine. Equipped with a six-speed manual gearbox, it offers aspiring competitors a relatively accessible entry into motorsport, with prices starting at €38,900 before VAT.

Above it sits the Ypsilon Rally4 HF, producing 212bhp and 290Nm from its turbocharged three-cylinder engine. Power reaches the front wheels through a five-speed SADEV sequential gearbox, with a mechanical limited-slip differential helping maximise traction. New examples start at €74,500 before VAT.

The flagship is the Ypsilon Rally2 HF Integrale, marking Lancia's return to the World Rally Championship's WRC2 category in 2026. Its 1.6-litre turbocharged four-cylinder produces 287bhp and 425Nm, delivered through a five-speed sequential gearbox and four-wheel drive. A ready-to-race example costs €268,000 before VAT, with an additional €41,000 equipment package available.

Interestingly, a small second-hand market is already developing. We found Rally4 examples advertised between approximately €65,000 and €78,000, while a lightly campaigned Rally2 HF Integrale has also appeared for sale, albeit without a published asking price.

These are serious competition machines, completely separate from the 280bhp electric Ypsilon HF road car, and a convincing demonstration that Lancia intends to rebuild its sporting reputation.


For our final rendering, we've taken the Delta's rallying potential one step further, imagining a privately entered competition car wearing a distinctive pink livery and The New Yardstick sponsorship.

The exaggerated wheel arches, competition equipment and striking colours give the design a character quite different from our understated road-going Delta. Yet the modern Lancia lighting signature and familiar proportions remain recognisable beneath the racing bodywork.

It's a fictional interpretation rather than an announced motorsport project, but one that demonstrates just how naturally the Delta name could return to the world that helped make it famous.


Lancia's revival is now moving beyond promises. With the new Gamma joining the Ypsilon, the brand is finally expanding into larger, more premium models for the first time in over a decade. Since the third-generation Delta ended production in 2014, the Ypsilon has carried Lancia almost single-handedly. The Gamma's arrival demonstrates that the company's ambitions are becoming reality, making the prospect of a fourth-generation Delta feel considerably more achievable.

There are further reasons for optimism. The HF badge has returned, Lancia is competing internationally once again, and its latest models demonstrate that the company is developing a recognisable design identity of its own.

Our fourth-generation Delta is simply an interpretation of what might come next: a stylish, practical family hatchback with a distinctly Italian personality, accompanied by the possibility of a much more powerful HF derivative.

For British enthusiasts, the story is particularly intriguing. A new Lancia might already be within reach through private importation, even if an official return to Britain remains uncertain.

The Delta has reinvented itself before. With the right timing, design and ambition, there's every reason to believe it could do so again.
