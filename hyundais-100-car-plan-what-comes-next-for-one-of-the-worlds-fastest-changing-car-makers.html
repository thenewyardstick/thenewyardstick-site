Hyundai's 100-Car Plan: What Comes Next for One of the World's Fastest-Changing Car Makers?

✪ Featured 8 Sep 2026

Twenty years ago, Hyundai was still fighting to prove that it could match the established European and Japanese brands. Today, that description feels almost unrecognisable. Hyundai has become a genuinely global manufacturer, producing everything from affordable small cars to sophisticated EVs, large SUVs and increasingly serious performance models.

Now it wants to grow again.

Hyundai has announced plans to launch or substantially refresh more than 100 vehicles globally by 2030, including 58 launches in North America, 49 in Korea and 41 in Europe. More importantly, many won't simply replace existing cars: Hyundai says it intends to enter new segments and target the ‘white spaces’ where its range is currently underrepresented.

The numbers are huge, but they do not mean Hyundai is about to offer the same enormous range everywhere. Increasingly, Hyundai is developing its line-up around individual markets: North America wants larger SUVs, hybrids and EREVs; India remains heavily focused on affordable, locally produced models; China demands increasingly competitive electrified products; while Europe needs smaller cars, efficient hybrids and EVs that work within tighter streets, emissions rules and household budgets. Hyundai itself says Europe will gain five all-new SUV and light-commercial vehicles as it pushes EV sales towards 420,000 annually by 2030.

The Inster is an easy car to like. Its funky styling gives it genuine character, while clever touches such as sliding rear seats help it extract surprising versatility from its tiny footprint. With up to 229 miles of WLTP range, Hyundai has also shown that a small EV needn't be restricted to short urban journeys.

But there is a problem: the Inster isn't particularly cheap for something so small. It evolved from the Korean-market petrol Casper rather than starting life as a clean-sheet European EV, and that becomes increasingly relevant as the competition develops. The larger Citroën ë-C3 and Fiat Grande Panda offer considerably more conventional family-car space, with both being offered with starting prices below that of the Inster.

Commercial vehicles are one of the most obvious ‘white spaces’ Hyundai could target in Europe, but it wouldn't actually be new territory for the brand. The old iLoad gave Hyundai a genuine medium-sized panel van in the UK, while the closely related i800 turned the same basic formula into an eight-seat passenger model. Both arrived when Hyundai had considerably less pulling power in Europe than it does today.

Our Staria-based proposal revisits that idea for a very different Hyundai. A panel van could put the badge back into the commercial market, while a passenger version could provide a genuinely spacious alternative to the SUVs that now dominate Hyundai's range.

There is also a much stronger industrial foundation behind the idea today. Kia's new PV5 already demonstrates what Hyundai Motor Group can do with a modern purpose-developed electric commercial vehicle, offered in both Cargo and Passenger forms.

The new Tucson is perhaps the clearest indication of how quickly Hyundai is moving. The international model has grown substantially, stretching to around 4,700mm long, with a longer wheelbase and a noticeably more substantial, upmarket presence.

Europe is likely to require a slightly different approach. Hyundai already produces different Tucson dimensions for different global markets, and a European version measuring around 4,600mm would make sense — still representing a meaningful increase over today's car without becoming unnecessarily large for European roads. For now, however, that figure should be treated as our expectation rather than a confirmed specification.

What's particularly striking is that the outgoing Tucson hardly feels old. The current generation only arrived in 2020 and received a substantial facelift for 2024, including an extensively redesigned interior.

Growing the Tucson creates another white space in Hyundai's European range. The Kona is 4,350mm long, while our expected European Tucson could approach 4,600mm. As the Tucson grows and moves further upmarket, the distance between Hyundai's two core SUVs becomes increasingly noticeable.

Kia demonstrates just how much room there is to work with here. In an even tighter section of the market, it has the XCeed, Niro and Sportage, each approaching the family crossover brief differently rather than simply duplicating one another.

That's where our Hyundai Sonoma comes in. Named after Sonoma, California, it follows the geographic naming convention already established by Kona, Tucson and Santa Fe. But rather than simply adding another SUV to the range, our Sonoma is designed to fill the space Hyundai's existing models are beginning to leave behind.

The Kona would remain the stylish, efficiency-led compact choice, while the incoming Tucson becomes larger, more sophisticated and increasingly upmarket. Sonoma would take the middle ground: a straightforward family SUV around the size of a Renault Austral, with hybrid power, a practical interior, a useful boot and an emphasis on affordability.

Hyundai's expansion doesn't have to be limited to filling practical gaps. The N performance brand has developed from a handful of hot versions of ordinary Hyundais into an increasingly important part of the company's identity, and Hyundai now wants global N sales to reach around 100,000 cars annually.

Reaching that scale creates room for N to become much broader. Cars such as the Ioniq 5 N and Ioniq 6 N can provide the volume, while more specialised models could build an identity that isn't entirely dependent on turning existing Hyundai models into faster ones.

Our N74 explores what that could look like. It draws inspiration from the 1974 Hyundai Pony Coupé Concept, the Giorgetto Giugiaro-designed wedge that has become an increasingly important part of Hyundai's design history. Hyundai has already revisited those ideas with the spectacular N Vision 74 concept; our proposal takes the next step, imagining those influences distilled into a production N sports car.

It wouldn't need enormous sales to justify its existence. Its job would be to give N a recognisable halo of its own, while making everything beneath it more desirable.

It is easy, particularly from a European perspective, to think of Hyundai simply as the Korean company behind cars such as the i10, Tucson and Ioniq 5. But that dramatically understates the scale of the organisation behind the badge.

Hyundai sits within a vast industrial and mobility ecosystem. Hyundai Motor Group encompasses Hyundai, Kia and Genesis, but its reach extends far beyond passenger cars. Hyundai Rotem builds trains, metros and high-speed rail technology; Hyundai Mobis develops components, electrification and vehicle technology; Hyundai produces buses and heavy commercial vehicles; and the group has invested heavily in hydrogen, software and future mobility. It also owns a controlling stake in robotics specialist Boston Dynamics.

That wider picture matters when considering Hyundai's 100-car plan. This isn't a relatively small manufacturer suddenly attempting an enormous expansion. Hyundai has access to manufacturing scale, battery and electrification expertise, commercial vehicles, robotics, software, hydrogen technology and an enormous global supply chain.

The transformation of its passenger cars over the past 20 years is impressive, but it represents only one part of what Hyundai has become. Increasingly, this is a global mobility and technology group with the resources to compete across almost every part of transportation.

That growing strength creates an awkward question for Genesis. Hyundai and Kia have both moved dramatically upmarket, with increasingly sophisticated interiors, distinctive designs and some of the group's most advanced electric technology. Simply offering something slightly more luxurious with a Genesis badge is becoming harder to justify.

The GV60 demonstrates the problem particularly clearly. It shares its E-GMP foundations with the Hyundai Ioniq 5 and Kia EV6, yet occupies a more expensive premium position. Meanwhile, Hyundai's new Ioniq 3 brings some surprisingly Genesis-like design themes to a much smaller and more affordable EV. The boundaries within the group are becoming increasingly blurred.

The pictured GV60 Magma should provide some separation. Yet even here, the numbers illustrate the challenge. It produces 641bhp and reaches 0–62mph in 3.4 seconds — exactly the same headline power and acceleration figures quoted for the Hyundai Ioniq 6 N.

That doesn't make the Magma a bad proposition; Genesis can differentiate it through materials, refinement, exclusivity and the ownership experience. But when Hyundai itself can produce something equally spectacular on paper, Genesis needs to offer more than additional luxury and a different badge.

The incoming GV90 points towards a much clearer future for Genesis. Rather than sitting slightly above an equivalent Hyundai or Kia, this is a genuine flagship: an enormous electric luxury SUV designed to give Genesis a presence at the very top of the market.

That immediately creates meaningful separation. The Hyundai Ioniq 9 and Kia EV9 are already large three-row electric SUVs, but they remain fundamentally spacious family cars. The GV90 goes considerably further, placing greater emphasis on rear-seat luxury, materials, technology and the sense of occasion expected from a flagship.

The range-topping GV90 Neolun demonstrates just how far Genesis is prepared to take that idea. Its pillarless coach-door arrangement has made the transition from concept to production, creating a huge opening into the cabin without a conventional fixed B-pillar. The standard GV90 retains conventional doors, leaving this deliberately extravagant feature for the flagship version.

This is where Genesis makes considerably more sense. It doesn't need to build a slightly more luxurious alternative to every Hyundai. Instead, it can use the group's enormous engineering resources to create cars that Hyundai and Kia simply don't offer.

An Ioniq 9 can be an excellent large family EV without threatening the GV90, because the two have fundamentally different jobs.

The G90 provides another example of where Genesis can operate without treading on Hyundai's toes. At more than 5.2 metres long in standard form, this is a genuinely enormous luxury saloon, with the space, technology and opulence to target traditional flagships such as the BMW 7 Series and Mercedes-Benz S-Class.

It isn't currently offered in the UK, where the G80 instead gives Genesis a large executive saloon alongside its SUV-heavy range, but the G90 demonstrates the potential above it.

This isn't an Ioniq 6 with nicer materials. The G90 is designed as much around being driven in as driving yourself, with huge rear-seat accommodation and a sense of occasion that Hyundai doesn't attempt to replicate.

That leaves the Ioniq 6 free to be a distinctive, relatively attainable electric saloon, while Genesis can go larger, more luxurious and considerably more opulent.

Alongside the GV90, it suggests the answer to the Genesis problem: don't sit just above Hyundai — give Genesis room to sit properly beyond it. Image credit: Genesis Motor


What Comes Next for Hyundai?

Hyundai's 100-car plan is ultimately about much more than launching an extraordinary number of new models. It shows how dramatically the company's position has changed. Hyundai can now fill gaps in its mainstream range, return to commercial vehicles, develop affordable EVs and hybrids, expand N and continue pushing its core models further upmarket.

But success creates its own challenges. As Hyundai becomes more desirable and technologically ambitious, the boundaries within Hyundai Motor Group become increasingly important — particularly for Genesis.

The opportunity is enormous. Hyundai doesn't need every new model to move upwards, and Genesis doesn't need to produce more expensive versions of cars that already exist elsewhere in the group. Give each brand and each new model a clear purpose, and those 100 launches could make Hyundai's range broader without making it more confusing.
