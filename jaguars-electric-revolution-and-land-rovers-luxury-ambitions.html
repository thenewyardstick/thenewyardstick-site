Jaguar's Electric Revolution and Land Rover's Luxury Ambitions

📝 Articles 3 Nov 2026

Looking at the wider Land Rover range, the scale of this transformation becomes even clearer. The Range Rover Evoque currently provides the most affordable entry point at £44,855, closely followed by the ageing Discovery Sport at £45,520. The latter is approaching retirement, with production scheduled to end in December 2026.

Without the Evoque and Velar, the cheapest model in today's line-up would be the three-door Defender 90, starting at £58,655. The larger Discovery begins at £69,540, while the Range Rover family increasingly occupies the upper reaches of the luxury market.

It is a remarkable change for a manufacturer once associated with practical, relatively attainable off-road vehicles.

Although smaller electric models could eventually fill some of these gaps, JLR's immediate direction is unmistakable. Much like Jaguar, its SUV brands are moving towards greater exclusivity, higher transaction prices and stronger profit margins.

The question is whether abandoning more affordable models will ultimately strengthen the business or narrow its appeal too far.

In 1997, the original Freelander provided an affordable entry into Land Rover ownership, followed by Freelander 2 in 2007 and the Discovery Sport Pure in 2017.

The 2027 figure is a prediction based on the planned withdrawal of the Discovery Sport, Range Rover Evoque and Velar. Assuming no cheaper replacements arrive, the Defender 90, currently priced from £58,655, could become the most affordable remaining model.

The Evoque and Velar are expected to continue through 2027, so this represents a possible future line-up rather than a confirmed 2027 starting price. All figures are approximate UK new-car list prices, excluding commercial variants, and have not been adjusted for inflation.


While the disappearance of Land Rover's more affordable models leaves a considerable gap in the range, one of JLR's most intriguing new arrivals is unlikely to solve that problem.

The new Range Rover GT takes an entirely different approach, introducing a sleek, low-slung grand tourer that moves the brand further into luxury territory.

Expected to arrive in early 2027, the GT will be built at JLR's extensively modernised Halewood factory in Merseyside, using the company's new Electrified Modular Architecture (EMA). It will launch exclusively with electric power, although a full-hybrid version is planned for later in its life.

Official technical specifications remain limited, but reports suggest a battery exceeding 100kWh, an 800-volt electrical system and rapid charging potentially approaching 350kW. Some estimates point towards a range approaching 500 miles, although such figures remain unconfirmed.


The interior has already been previewed, introducing a minimalist dashboard, discreet air vents, a central touchscreen and generous rear passenger accommodation. Its exterior, meanwhile, promises something closer to a raised luxury saloon than a traditional Range Rover SUV.

Pricing is expected to begin at approximately £100,000 for the electric model, with a subsequent hybrid potentially lowering the entry point towards £90,000.

Despite earlier speculation, the GT is not a direct replacement for the outgoing Velar. Range Rover has confirmed that it was conceived as a separate addition to the family, offering a more road-focused interpretation of the brand.

Our Design Studio renderings explore how this distinctive new direction could translate into its final production appearance.


The Freelander name, however, is making an unexpected comeback. JLR has partnered with Chinese manufacturer Chery to revive it as a standalone electrified brand, with the new Freelander 8 already launched in China.

Built using Chery technology with design input from JLR, the revived Freelander operates independently of Range Rover, Defender and Discovery. A UK launch has also been confirmed, although British pricing and an arrival date remain uncertain.

Whether this new generation can recapture the original Freelander's relatively affordable positioning remains to be seen.

Another possible solution may arrive through the rumoured Defender Sport. Expected to sit below the Defender family, it could eventually inherit some of the Discovery Sport's role, although pricing and launch timing remain uncertain.


If Jaguar is determined to become a £100,000-plus luxury brand, does it really need another SUV, or would a beautiful electric coupé be a more convincing way to reconnect with its past?

Few manufacturers possess a sporting heritage quite like Jaguar. The E-Type's dramatic proportions, the XK's effortless grand-touring elegance and the F-Type's muscular presence have helped define the brand across generations.

An electric successor need not imitate any of them. Instead, a beautifully proportioned two-door could reinterpret that identity for a new era, while occupying a space comfortably removed from Range Rover and Defender.

With the Type 01 already establishing Jaguar's new four-door luxury ambitions, our rendering imagines another possibility: a modern electric coupé that looks confidently towards the future without forgetting what made Jaguar special in the first place.


Jaguar knows how to make a limited-edition, high-end model feel genuinely special. The XE SV Project 8 was proof: 600PS from a supercharged V8, just 300 examples worldwide and a price approaching £150,000. It was never about chasing sales volume, but creating something extraordinary.

That is the challenge facing JLR today. Range Rover is becoming more exclusive, Defender and Discovery are being repositioned, and Jaguar is stepping into a world of six-figure electric luxury.

The strategy could bring stronger profit margins, but it also risks distancing the company from customers who helped build its reputation.

Perhaps the answer isn't necessarily to make every brand more affordable, but to give each one a clearer identity. For Jaguar, that might mean embracing the beauty and excitement of a great coupé, rather than following its stablemates into an increasingly crowded luxury SUV market.

Ultimately, JLR's future will depend not simply on how much its cars cost, but whether they offer something genuinely worth paying for.
