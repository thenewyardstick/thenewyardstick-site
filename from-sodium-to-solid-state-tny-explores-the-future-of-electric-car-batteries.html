From Sodium to Solid-State: TNY Explores the Future of Electric Car Batteries

✪ Featured 15 Nov 2026

The Volvo EX60 P12 AWD uses a high-nickel nickel-manganese-cobalt (NMC) lithium-ion battery, with a conventional liquid electrolyte rather than a solid-state design. Its cathode combines nickel, manganese and cobalt, while the anode is graphite-based.

The pack has a 117kWh gross capacity, of which 112kWh is usable, and operates on an 800V electrical architecture. Volvo claims up to 503 miles of WLTP range, supported by a dual-motor, 680hp powertrain.

The complete vehicle has a published running order mass of approximately 2,405kg; Volvo has not published a separately verified figure for the complete battery pack's own weight.


Replacing the conventional graphite anode with a silicon-rich alternative could make the EX60's battery both lighter and more energy-dense. Silicon can store considerably more lithium than graphite, potentially allowing the existing 112kWh usable pack to be reduced in weight. Our estimates suggest a potential saving of around 30–80kg, while retaining the P12's 503-mile WLTP range.

Alternatively, Volvo could use the same battery weight and physical space to accommodate more energy. A pack offering around 118–123kWh usable capacity could potentially push the P12 towards 530–553 miles, assuming unchanged vehicle efficiency.

We estimate the current NMC pack would cost around $15,000–17,000 to manufacture, compared with approximately $16,000–19,000 for this higher-capacity silicon-rich alternative.

However, silicon expands significantly during charging, placing stress on the anode and potentially accelerating degradation. Maintaining fast charging, safety and long-term durability remains a major engineering challenge.


Semi-solid-state batteries replace some of the conventional liquid electrolyte with a gel-like or solid electrolyte, potentially improving energy density, thermal stability and safety. Applied to the EX60, this could allow the existing 112kWh usable battery to become substantially lighter, with our estimates suggesting a potential saving of around 60–140kg.

Alternatively, Volvo could use a similar battery weight and physical footprint to accommodate around 120–125kWh of usable energy, potentially increasing the P12's WLTP range to approximately 539–562 miles.

However, this technology remains expensive to manufacture at automotive scale. We estimate a potential pack cost of $29,000–38,000, compared with $15,000–17,000 for conventional NMC.

Although the electrolyte could improve thermal stability, maintaining consistent performance, long-term durability and economical mass production remain significant challenges.


An all-solid-state battery could represent a much bigger step forward for the EX60, replacing its liquid electrolyte with a solid alternative and potentially using a lithium-metal anode. This could substantially increase energy density, allowing the existing 112kWh usable pack to become around 50–170kg lighter while retaining the P12's 503-mile WLTP range.

Alternatively, Volvo could use a similar battery weight and physical footprint to accommodate approximately 125–130kWh of usable energy, potentially taking the P12 towards 562–584 miles.

However, early-production costs could be substantial, with our estimates putting a complete pack at around $47,000–94,000.

The technology promises impressive energy density and potentially improved safety, but challenges remain around manufacturing consistency, maintaining contact between solid materials, lithium dendrite formation and long-term durability. Mass production at competitive prices remains a significant hurdle.

Optimising the existing LFP chemistry could be one of the most straightforward ways to improve the Frontera's battery without introducing an entirely new material. Advances in electrode coatings, particle sizing, cell manufacturing and battery-pack integration could increase energy density while reducing production costs. Better cell-to-pack designs could also reduce the amount of supporting structure required around the cells.

The existing 44kWh pack could potentially become around 10–20kg lighter, with manufacturing improvements bringing its estimated cost down from around $3,300–3,800 to $2,900–3,400. With capacity unchanged, the Frontera would retain its 186-mile WLTP range, although a small efficiency improvement could take this towards 190 miles. 

Alternatively, using the weight and packaging savings to accommodate a larger battery could potentially increase capacity to around 46–48kWh, lifting range to approximately 194–203 miles.

The advantage is that LFP already benefits from an established supply chain, relatively abundant materials and good durability. However, improvements are likely to be incremental rather than revolutionary, and LFP will still have a lower energy-density ceiling than more advanced lithium-ion chemistries. 


LMFP builds on LFP by replacing some of the iron in its cathode with manganese. This increases the battery's operating voltage, allowing it to store more energy without relying on expensive nickel and cobalt. It retains many of the cost and thermal-stability advantages associated with phosphate-based batteries, while potentially delivering around 10–20% greater energy density than conventional LFP.

With greater energy density, LMFP could make a 44kWh battery around 255–295kg, compared with an estimated 285–330kg for the existing LFP pack. We estimate a pack cost of approximately $3,000–3,600. Keeping capacity unchanged would leave the Frontera close to its existing 186-mile range, but using the improved density to increase capacity to around 48–51kWh could potentially deliver 203–216 miles.

That would offer a meaningful improvement without moving to a conventional nickel-rich NMC battery. However, manganese introduces additional challenges. Slower lithium-ion movement, reduced electrical conductivity and manganese dissolution can affect charging performance and long-term durability. Manufacturers must overcome these issues before LMFP can realise its full potential at scale.  


Sodium-ion batteries take a different approach, replacing lithium with sodium, an abundant and widely available material. Our example uses a Prussian-white cathode and a hard-carbon anode, with aluminium current collectors on both sides of the cell. This reduces reliance on several critical battery materials and could eventually offer manufacturers a cheaper alternative to lithium-based chemistries. 

Sodium-ion presents a different compromise. Its estimated 44kWh pack could weigh around 295–350kg, with a potential cost of $3,000–3,700. That means it may not immediately offer a weight or price advantage over LFP.

At the same capacity, the Frontera could still achieve a broadly comparable range, although its lower energy density could make it heavier. If the same weight and packaging allowance were retained, a more realistic capacity might be around 40–44kWh, giving an estimated 170–186 miles. 

Its appeal would instead lie in material availability, supply-chain diversification and potential cold-weather performance. However, the technology needs further development and greater manufacturing scale before it can become a commercially competitive alternative

What Does the Future Hold for EV Batteries?

The next generation of battery technology could change electric cars in ways that go far beyond extending their driving range.

For affordable models such as the Vauxhall Frontera, improvements to established LFP chemistry, the arrival of LMFP and the development of sodium-ion could help reduce costs, improve efficiency and make electric motoring more accessible. At the premium end, silicon-rich anodes and solid-state technologies could allow cars such as the Volvo EX60 to carry lighter battery packs, travel further or achieve both.

However, progress will not happen overnight. Manufacturing costs, material availability, charging performance, durability and the ability to produce cells at enormous scale will determine which technologies make it into mainstream vehicles. Some advances are already moving towards production, while others remain years away from widespread adoption.

Ultimately, the most successful battery will not necessarily be the one with the highest energy density. It will be the one that delivers the best balance of price, range, weight, reliability and practicality for the driver. And as battery technology continues to evolve, that balance could make electric cars an increasingly compelling choice.
