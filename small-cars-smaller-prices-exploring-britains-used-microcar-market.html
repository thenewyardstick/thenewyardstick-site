Small Cars, Smaller Prices? Exploring Britain's Used Microcar Market

📈 Market Watch 9 Nov 2026

The Aixam Coupé offers something increasingly unusual in our selection: compact urban motoring without a charging cable. Its two-cylinder Kubota diesel and CVT automatic will never win any races, but for short commutes, shopping trips and tight parking spaces, that simplicity has appeal.

Unlike the electric Ami, Twizy and Microlino, it can refuel at any diesel pump, making it particularly relevant to drivers without convenient home charging.

UK availability is limited. When we checked Auto Trader, just two 2013 diesel Coupés were advertised: a privately sold example with 21,800 miles at £4,350, and a lower-mileage dealer car with around 14,100 miles at £5,995. These are asking prices, not a reliable national average, and condition matters.

Fuel costs should remain modest for a low-mileage urban commuter, even with today's diesel prices. Insurance is another matter for younger drivers: our illustrative comprehensive quote for a 17-year-old came to £1,997.75. With a top speed of around 28mph, the Coupé remains very much a local runabout.

At just 2.41 metres long, the Citroën Ami is perhaps the purest expression of the modern urban microcar. Its two-seat cabin, tight turning circle and 28mph top speed make it particularly suited to short shopping trips, crowded streets and small parking spaces. Classified as a light quadricycle rather than a conventional car, motorway use is prohibited.

Used prices are becoming increasingly attractive, with examples advertised from around £4,600 when we checked. Its official range is up to 46 miles, while a full charge takes approximately four hours. For drivers covering relatively short distances with access to inexpensive home charging, electricity costs can be remarkably low.

Insurance is the catch for younger buyers. Our illustrative comprehensive quote for a 17-year-old came to £2,745.59, considerably more than the figure obtained for our experienced driver. Ultimately, the Ami makes most sense when its limited speed and range comfortably fit your everyday routine.

Certain versions of the Microcar M.Go offer something unusual among microcars: four seats. Its 505cc petrol engine and CVT automatic gearbox offer a practical alternative to the two-seat Aixam Coupé and Citroën Ami, particularly for short local journeys.

Crucially, this four-seat version is a heavy L7e quadricycle, rather than an L6e light quadricycle. It therefore cannot be driven at 16 on a category AM licence; drivers generally need a full category B car licence. Unlike 28mph L6e models, it can also reach higher speeds, although its lightweight construction deserves respect on faster roads. Like other quadricycles, it does not offer conventional-car levels of crash protection.

With examples advertised from around £2,500, it is among the cheapest vehicles to buy in our selection. Petrol costs should remain reasonable for urban commuters travelling just 3,000 miles annually. Insurance is another consideration: our illustrative comprehensive quotation for a 17-year-old came to £1,975.92.

The Microlino might be the most charming vehicle in our selection, but it is hardly the cheapest way into microcar ownership. Inspired by the classic BMW Isetta, its front-opening door, upright cabin and distinctive proportions make even an ordinary shopping trip feel like an occasion.

Unlike the Citroën Ami, the Microlino is an L7e heavy quadricycle capable of around 56mph, with seating for two and a surprisingly useful 230-litre boot.

We're looking at the 15kWh Long Range version, offering up to 142 miles between charges. Used 2025 examples have appeared from around £17,000, enough to buy a considerably more versatile conventional electric car. This makes the Microlino a purchase driven by affection as much as logic.

Electricity costs should be minimal for an urban commuter charging at home. Insurance is less forgiving for younger owners, however: our illustrative comprehensive quote for a 17-year-old came to £2,636.87. For enthusiasts who value personality over outright practicality, its appeal is obvious.

Few microcars have as much personality as the Renault Twizy. Its tandem seats, exposed sides and upward-opening doors make it feel more like an electric scooter with four wheels than a conventional car.

The UK-market Twizy 80 is an L7e heavy quadricycle, capable of 50mph and normally requiring a full car licence from 17. Renault claimed up to 62 miles per charge, although 37–50 miles is a more realistic everyday expectation.

Used prices now start around £4,000–£4,500, but battery ownership deserves particular attention. Many early Twizys were sold with a separate battery lease, historically costing around £50 a month — approximately £600 annually.

Some batteries have since been purchased outright, so buyers should check the current ownership arrangement even when an advert says "Battery Lease". Buyout prices vary, and a written settlement quotation should be obtained from Mobilize Financial Services rather than assuming a fixed purchase price. For young drivers, insurance could be the surprise attraction: our illustrative comprehensive quote for a 17-year-old was just £1,208.65.

The Fiat 500 provides an interesting comparison with our microcars. Despite its compact dimensions, it offers four seats, a useful boot and considerably more flexibility for longer journeys. Used 2016 examples with around 40,000 miles can be found for approximately £4,000, putting them directly alongside some of our featured microcars.

The familiar 69bhp 1.2-litre petrol engine has a manufacturer-claimed combined economy figure of 60.1mpg, while annual vehicle tax is just £20. At our assumed petrol price of 173.9p per litre, covering 3,000 miles would cost approximately £394.62 in fuel.

Our illustrative comprehensive insurance quote for a 30-year-old driver was £464.46, bringing estimated annual fuel, insurance and tax costs to £879.08, excluding maintenance.

For buyers who don't specifically need a microcar, the Fiat makes a compelling alternative. It remains small enough for urban use while offering considerably greater versatility, particularly for families and drivers regularly venturing beyond town.


The Smart Fortwo Electric Drive is a fascinating halfway point between a microcar and a conventional electric car. Measuring just 2.69 metres long, it is remarkably easy to park, yet offers proper car classification and a maximum speed of 80mph. Used 2017 examples can be found from around £7,000.

Its biggest limitation is range. Smart originally claimed 99 miles from the 17.6kWh battery, considerably less than the Microlino Long Range's 142-mile figure. Real-world range will generally be lower, particularly during winter.

Electricity costs are impressively modest. Using Smart's official consumption figure and our 9p overnight charging tariff, 3,000 miles would cost approximately £56.05.

However, annual tax is £200 for examples registered from April 2017, while our comprehensive insurance quote was £582.62. That produces an estimated annual total of £838.67. For urban drivers wanting something tiny without the compromises of quadricycle ownership, the Smart deserves consideration.

Microcars are hardly a new invention. Long before the Citroën Ami, Renault Twizy and Microlino arrived, manufacturers were experimenting with tiny, economical vehicles designed to make everyday transport more accessible.

The BMW Isetta is perhaps the most recognisable example. We found a 1961 Isetta 300 advertised in Britain for £14,995, making it one of the most expensive vehicles in our selection, despite its age.

As a historic vehicle, it qualifies for free road tax, while specialist classic-car insurance could prove surprisingly affordable. However, fuel costs are less predictable, and maintaining a 65-year-old vehicle could require considerably more attention and expense. The Isetta is ultimately a charming classic to enjoy rather than a particularly sensible daily commuter.

So, are used microcars genuinely cheap to run?

Our comparison suggests that electric microcars have a clear advantage when it comes to everyday running costs. With inexpensive home charging and no vehicle tax, the Ami, Twizy and Microlino can offer remarkably affordable urban transport. The Twizy looks particularly attractive, provided its battery is owned outright.

Nevertheless, conventional small cars aren't necessarily far behind. The Fiat 500 and electric Smart Fortwo demonstrate that relatively inexpensive motoring doesn't require sacrificing quite so much practicality, speed or versatility.

But microcars aren't always purchased simply to save money. For some drivers, their tiny dimensions, effortless parking and suitability for short journeys are the real attractions. Others may specifically need a vehicle that can be driven on a category AM licence.

Ultimately, the best choice depends on how, where and why the vehicle will be used. For someone whose daily driving rarely ventures beyond their local town, a microcar could be all the transport they need.
